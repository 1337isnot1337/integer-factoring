# Normalized circuit averages without count-dependent precision

## Constructive theorem

Fix a positive number of variables `d`. Let `q` be an integer arithmetic
circuit, of unrestricted formal degree, and let

$$
\mathcal B=\prod_{i=1}^d[L_i,U_i)\cap\mathbb Z^d,
\qquad m_i=U_i-L_i>0.
$$

Signed endpoints are allowed. Define the normalized complete average

$$
\Lambda_{\mathcal B}(q)=
\frac{2^d}{\prod_i m_i}\sum_{a\in\mathcal B}q(a). \tag{1}
$$

This scalar is integral in `Z_2`. Given precision `p>=1`, it is
computable modulo `2^p` in time polynomial in the written circuit,
the endpoint bit lengths, and `p`, for fixed `d`. The coefficient
quotient works at **exactly `p` bits**, independently of
`sum_i v_2(m_i)`. No numerical interval or full coefficient list for
`q` is expanded.

The theorem also permits an `inv` gate whenever its input is an odd
unit at every integer parity tuple. This condition is checked at all
`2^d` parity tuples for every inverse gate. The allowed circuits include
regular rational functions with very large formal numerator and
denominator degrees. The quotient inversion and its passage to the
actual normalized sum are justified in section 5.

The normalization in (1) is a known input cardinality. This is an
actual normalization algorithm, but it does not divide away an unknown
singular product content or evaluate a denominator such as `N-ab` at
its factor poles. The unrestricted factor-count scalar and any complete
factoring exponent above `1/2` remain open.

This extends the [circuit box product](circuit-box-products.md) and its
quotient arithmetic to a new, normalized functional. Unlike retaining
an ordinary sum to `p+v_2(|B|)` bits and dividing afterward, the circuit
evaluation itself uses only `p` coefficient bits. The small basis
functionals still require polynomial arithmetic in the endpoint bit
lengths, which is charged below. The proof and implementation are
AI-assisted ordinary work, without new Lean formalization.

## 1. A finite average of a falling factorial

Write

$$
(X)_t=X(X-1)\cdots(X-t+1),\qquad (X)_0=1,
$$

and first use one interval `[0,m)`. The binomial summation identity gives

$$
\Lambda_m((X)_t)
=\frac2m\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(i)_t
=\frac{2t!}{t+1}\binom{m-1}{t}. \tag{2}
$$

For `t>=m`, both sides are zero. Consequently

$$
v_2\bigl(\Lambda_m((X)_t)\bigr)
\ge\lambda_t,
\qquad
\lambda_t=1+v_2(t!)-v_2(t+1). \tag{3}
$$

Equality holds at `m=t+1`, so this universal lower bound is exact.

Every `lambda_t` is nonnegative. Indeed, if `a=v_2(t+1)>=1`, then
`t>=2^a-1`, and

$$
1+v_2(t!)\ge1+v_2((2^a-1)!)=2^a-a\ge a.
$$

The case `a=0` is immediate. Since the falling factorials are a monic
integer basis for integer-coefficient polynomials, (2)--(3) prove

$$
\Lambda_m(\mathbb Z[X])\subseteq\mathbb Z_2. \tag{4}
$$

In particular, dividing a complete polynomial sum by a large power of
two in its length loses at most the one normalization bit already
supplied by the factor two. Statement (4) is not a claim that arbitrary
integer-valued functions have this property.

## 2. The exact universal falling-factorial quotient

Let

$$
D(p)=1+\max\{t\ge0:\lambda_t<p\}. \tag{5}
$$

This threshold is finite and at most `8p`. To see this, use

$$
\lambda_t=1+t-s_2(t)-v_2(t+1)
\ge1+t-2\log_2(t+1).
$$

The right side is increasing for `t>=8`. The elementary inequality
`8p+1<=2^(3p+1)` implies its value at `t=8p` is at least `2p-1>=p`.
Thus all failing indices lie below `8p`, and a scan of those `8p`
integers computes the exact threshold using binary digit counts.

Multiplication by `X` preserves the integer span of the falling
factorials with indices at least `D`, because

$$
X(X)_t=(X)_{t+1}+t(X)_t.
$$

Therefore every multiple `(X)_D g(X)`, for `g` in `Z[X]`, expands as
an integer linear combination of `(X)_t` with `t>=D`. Equations
(3)--(5) give

$$
\boxed{\Lambda_m((X)_{D(p)}g(X))\equiv0\pmod{2^p}
\quad\text{for every }m\ge1\text{ and }g\in\mathbb Z[X].} \tag{6}
$$

Hence every normalized interval functional factors through

$$
\mathcal A_p=(\mathbb Z/2^p)[X]/((X)_{D(p)}). \tag{7}
$$

This is a functional identity. Its proof charges the normalization
before reducing the circuit; it does not divide a pointwise modular
congruence by an unaccounted large power of two.

The degree in (5) is optimal among universal quotients generated by a
single initial falling factorial. If `D'<D(p)`, choose a failing
`t>=D'`, take `m=t+1`, and write
`(X)_t=(X)_(D') prod_(D'<=j<t)(X-j)`. Its normalized average has
valuation exactly `lambda_t<p` by (2). Thus the ideal generated by
`(X)_(D')` does not annihilate every interval average modulo `2^p`.
No optimality claim is made for arbitrary quotient representations.

## 3. Evaluate the circuit before applying the functional

The quotient polynomial in (7) is monic of degree `D=O(p)`. Evaluate
the supplied circuit in this quotient, retaining `D` coefficients of
`p` bits after every gate. Coefficient reduction modulo `2^p` is safe
because every basis functional is integral by (4). Monic polynomial
reduction is safe because of the full-ideal identity (6).

The first `D` basis values are computed exactly from ordinary power
sums:

$$
\ell_j=\frac2m\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}i^j,\qquad0\le j<D. \tag{8}
$$

Writing `m=2^v u`, with `u` odd, the numerator `2 sum i^j` is divisible
by `2^v` by (4). Compute the power sum as an exact integer, divide by
that known power of two, and multiply by `u^-1 mod 2^p`. Prefix power
sums through degree `D-1` use polynomially many operations on integers
of `O(D log(m+1)+D log D)` bits. The numerical value of `m` is never
an iteration count.

If the reduced circuit is `sum_(j<D)c_j X^j`, return
`sum c_j ell_j mod 2^p`. For an interval beginning at `L`, evaluate the
circuit with its variable replaced by `L+X` in the same quotient.
Translation preserves integral polynomial coefficients, so (6) still
applies.

## 4. Fixed-dimensional boxes

For `d` variables, use one copy of (7) per coordinate. The basis has
`D(p)^d` monomials. The functional of a basis monomial separates into
the product of its one-variable normalized averages:

$$
\Lambda_{\mathcal B}\left(\prod_i(X_i-L_i)^{e_i}\right)
=\prod_i\left(\frac2{m_i}\sum_{j=0}^{m_i-1}j^{e_i}\right). \tag{9}
$$

Each factor is integral. The ideal generated by `(X_i)_(D(p))` in
any coordinate is annihilated by (1), using (6) term by term in the
remaining coordinates. The same circuit-evaluation argument therefore
proves the box theorem with coefficient precision `p`, independent of
the sum of count valuations.

The complexity is polynomial for fixed `d`; the explicit basis has
size `D(p)^d`, and global unit verification below uses `2^d` parity
tuples. No polynomial bound uniform in a growing number of variables
is asserted. Dividing the returned residue by `2^d` gives the ordinary
average to absolute precision `p-d`; request `p+d` bits when the
ordinary average itself is needed to absolute precision `p`.

## 5. Globally odd-unit inverses are valid in this quotient

An inverse gate is permitted only if its input is odd at every parity
tuple in `{0,1}^d`. Earlier inverse inputs must satisfy the same
condition. Reducing the circuit modulo two checks all of them without
visiting the box. This condition is stronger than being a unit at
only the finitely many nodes of one short interval; the implementation
rejects a gate that fails it.

First, such an input is a unit in the quotient. Modulo two, the ideal
of functions vanishing on `{0,1}^d` is generated by `X_i(X_i-1)`.
The reduction of a globally odd input is `1+n`, with `n` in that
ideal. If `2^r>=D`, Frobenius gives `n^(2^r)=0` modulo all the falling
factorial relations: in one variable, `(X_i)_D` reduces to a product
of powers of `X_i` and `X_i-1`, each with multiplicity at most `D`.
Thus

$$
u^{2^r}\equiv1\pmod2
\quad\text{in the quotient.}
$$

Further squaring lifts this to one modulo `2^p`. For example,
`u^(2^(r+p)-1)` is an inverse modulo `2^p`. Ordinary binary powering
uses `O(p+log D)` quotient multiplications. This also handles units
such as `1+X+X^2`, whose coefficients are not constant modulo two
even though the polynomial is odd at every integer.

Second, this quotient inverse computes the actual normalized rational
sum; this does not follow merely by writing an inverse symbol.
Approximate every globally odd inverse by sufficiently large ordinary
integer powers. These polynomial circuits converge uniformly on
integer tuples to the rational circuit. In the finite quotient, their
values eventually equal the constructed inverse exactly. For any
fixed encoded box, the normalized finite sum is a continuous linear
functional over the two-adic field. Its possible count-dependent loss
is finite, so the normalized averages of the polynomial approximants
converge to the desired rational average. Every polynomial average is
integral by (4) and (9), and their residues are eventually the same
quotient output. This proves both integrality and correctness for the
rational circuit, without allocating that count-dependent precision
in the algorithm.

There is no corresponding permission to invert a nonunit such as a
factor pole. Its polynomial approximants and quotient inverses used
above do not exist under the stated contract.

## 6. Exact controls

Files:

- [Normalized-average evaluator](../tools/circuit_box_averages.py).
- [Independent checker](../tools/check_circuit_box_averages.py).
- [Receipt](../evidence/circuit-box-averages.json).

The checker computes the sharp degree threshold at each precision
from one through 48. Every smaller-degree witness is checked by a
literal normalized sum. Forty-eight further ideal-multiple checks
use literal integer sums. Eighty-eight polynomial circuits and 100
regular rational controls are compared with direct modular values
retained at the full count-dependent precision before exact division.
The tests include negative endpoints, coupled variables, nilpotent
unit inverses, and rejection of a denominator that is a unit only on
the requested singleton interval.

Two independently specified large polynomial examples exercise the
removed precision loss:

- On an interval of length `3*2^400`, the 501-node circuit
  `X^(2^500)` has normalized average one modulo `2^16`. The evaluator
  uses a degree-20 quotient at 16 bits, despite the 400 count-valuation
  bits. The raw sum is zero at that precision.
- On a box of side lengths `3*2^100` and `5*2^150`, the 303-node
  circuit `(X+Y)^(2^300)` has normalized average two modulo `2^8`.
  Its quotient has degree 12 in each variable and dimension 144;
  no additional 250 count-valuation bits are used.

In each case, the large even power is zero on even arguments and one
on odd arguments at a precision exceeding the full reference
normalization loss. Exactly half the arguments are odd, which proves
the independently expected residues. A further large regular-rational
control evaluates `1/(1+2X^(2^500))` on the first interval. Its normalized
average is `4/3 mod 2^16`, computed with the same 16-bit quotient.

```sh
python3 tools/check_circuit_box_averages.py --output /tmp/circuit-box-averages.json
cmp /tmp/circuit-box-averages.json evidence/circuit-box-averages.json
```

These are actual normalized sums. Their denominator is the known box
cardinality and their circuit denominators are globally units. The
factor-count problem has an additional singular correlation outside
these hypotheses, so the complete factoring score remains `1/2`.
